# Conduct a review

Start with the decision behind the request. Recover the intended outcome and the actual target from the supplied material before asking for more. A plan describes future behavior; distinguish predictions from observations. A review brief can include accepted decisions without including the author's transcript.

Choose the most consequential thing that could make the work fail its purpose. Follow it from promise to evidence to user or operational effect. Widen scope when a caller, another section, or an end-to-end journey changes the answer. Use the [evidence guides](references/evidence-guides.md) for the target at hand. Use the applicable playbook when a team standard, decision source, or contact route matters.

For a disputed design, give its best grounded justification, compare the simplest credible alternative, and identify what each costs or protects. Check a claim directly where practical. A well-supported risk may matter without a runnable reproduction; a scanner alert or a confident reply still needs investigation. Ask an available decision owner a narrow question if their answer could change the conclusion. In a one-shot assignment, state the question and finish the useful work.

Give the requester a scoped recommendation unless they asked for facts only. For a material finding, identify the place, evidence, practical consequence, and what would resolve it. Distinguish a demonstrated problem from a decision that remains open. Note significant coverage limits. Stop when the requester has enough to act; a review has no finding quota or mandatory set of passes.

For an explicit fix check, recover the earlier finding and revision, then test the old failure against the new work. Say which concerns remain, are resolved, have been superseded, or cannot yet be judged. If asked for a fresh second opinion, form it without the prior verdict.
